# Writing Style

The full guide is [writing_style.md](./references/writing_style.md). Read it before writing anything substantial. The examples are the useful part; they give you a feel for the voice in a way a list of rules can't.

## The one-paragraph version

Give the reader some orientation early, then say the interesting thing plainly. Let the reasoning show instead of writing as if the answer was obvious all along. Look for the tension, the assumption, the mechanism, or the tradeoff underneath the thing. Use concrete cases when they help, be honest about uncertainty, and keep the voice conversational even when the ideas are serious.

> Don't write like someone who already knows the answer. Write like someone who found something interesting and is trying to figure out exactly what it means.

## How to use it

1. Read the guide, not just the short version.
2. Write the thing.
3. Reread against sections 13, 15, and 16. Manufactured profundity, technical-sounding prose, and generic polish are the habits most likely to creep back in.

## A few mechanical things the guide implies

- Parentheses or commas, not em-dashes.
- Use prose when the content is an argument and tables when it's actually tabular.
- In research writing, say what was tried, what happened, and what it means. State negative results plainly.
- In notebooks, use short explanations between cells and start with the smallest example that shows the idea.
- No pitch writing, ever.
- In READMEs and docs, say what a thing is before referring to it by name, keep the README short and link into the docs for the mechanism, and don't open every doc (or PR) the same way.

## Keeping it current

The guide is meant to change as we learn. If I keep rewriting the same kind of sentence or asking you to stop doing something, the guide is probably missing a preference. Point that out and offer to update it.
